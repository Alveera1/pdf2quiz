1. Explain the role and sequence of the ETL process in a data warehouse, including how it handles data from multiple sources with differing formats, and why each step is crucial for the integrity of the warehouse.
   Answer: The ETL process in a data warehouse consists of three sequential steps: Extract, Transform, and Load. First, the Extract step collects data from various source systems such as sales applications, websites, ERP and CRM databases, and spreadsheets. This step must capture all relevant records, even if they come in different formats or structures. Second, the Transform step cleans, standardizes, validates, joins, filters, and converts the extracted data into a consistent, warehouse‑ready format. For example, sales data from three branches might use different date formats and currency representations; the Transform step converts these into a common date format and a single currency before further processing. This step ensures data quality, consistency, and integration across sources. Third, the Load step stores the transformed data into the warehouse’s central repository, typically in a historical, read‑optimized form. Each step is crucial: extraction ensures completeness, transformation guarantees data integrity and consistency, and loading places the data into a structure suitable for efficient analytical querying. Together, these steps maintain the reliability and usefulness of the data warehouse for reporting and decision‑making.

2. Describe the differences between a star schema and a snowflake schema in dimensional modeling, including how normalization affects redundancy and query performance.
   Answer: A star schema and a snowflake schema are both dimensional modeling techniques used to organize data for efficient analysis. In a star schema, there is one central fact table that connects directly to several dimension tables. Each dimension table is denormalized, meaning it contains all attributes in a single table. This design is simple, reduces the number of joins required during queries, and is therefore well suited for fast, straightforward analytical queries. However, the denormalized nature can lead to redundancy because common attributes may be repeated across rows. In contrast, a snowflake schema extends the star schema by further normalizing dimension tables into related sub‑tables. This reduces redundancy by storing shared attributes only once, but it increases the number of joins required during queries. As a result, while a snowflake schema can save storage space and maintain data integrity, it may require more complex queries and can be slower for some analytical workloads compared to the simpler star schema.

3. Compare and contrast OLTP and OLAP systems in terms of purpose, data characteristics, operations, query types, and user base, and explain why a data warehouse is suited for analytical workloads rather than daily transactions.
   Answer: OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) systems and OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) systems serve distinct purposes and exhibit different characteristics. OLTP systems are designed for daily transactions, handling current data that is frequently inserted, updated, or deleted. Their operations are short and frequent, and the database design is usually normalized to avoid redundancy and maintain data integrity. The users of OLTP systems are typically clerks and applications that require quick, transactional access. In contrast, OLAP systems, often implemented within a data warehouse, are meant for analysis and decision support. They store both current and historical data, which allows for time‑variant analysis. Operations in OLAP are mostly read/query oriented, with complex analytical queries that aggregate data across multiple dimensions. OLAP databases are often dimensional or denormalized to speed up query performance. Because OLAP focuses on read‑heavy analytical workloads and supports complex queries over large historical datasets, a data warehouse is specifically structured to support these needs, whereas OLTP systems are optimized for transaction throughput and data consistency in real‑time operations.
